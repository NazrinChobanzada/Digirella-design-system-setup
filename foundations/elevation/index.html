Elevation New

tokens/elevation.css · Shadow is a signal, not decoration. Cards are flat by default; shadow means interaction or layering.

PrimitiveSemanticWhat reads it
--shadow-none--elevation-flatEverything by default. Not the absence of a token — the flat level is a choice.
--shadow-100--elevation-raisedSwitch knob, slider thumb, the selected item in a segmented control, a raised card.
--shadow-200--elevation-stickyThe top bar once content scrolls under it, sticky table headers.
--shadow-300--elevation-overlayMenu, popover, tooltip, every select and combobox list, an interactive card on hover.
--shadow-400--elevation-modal
--elevation-transient		Dialog and drawer. Toast takes the same value and clears them by --z-toast.

Read the middle column, not the left one. A component asks for a behaviour — --elevation-overlay — and the primitive it resolves to is an implementation detail that can be retuned without touching a single component. --shadow-inset is separate again: it is a highlight along a top edge, not a level.

Four values draw something (100 through 400), plus none. They back six behaviours, because flat uses none and modal and transient share 400 — those two are separated by stacking order rather than by a heavier shade. So: four shadows, six levels, and the two numbers are not supposed to match.

Guidelines

Six behaviours, four values

A level is a behaviour, not a depth: what the surface is allowed to do. Naming them after behaviours is what stops a shadow being picked because it looks nice.

There are six behaviours and four shadow values, so two pairs share one — and where they share, the difference is carried by stacking order instead. That is a compression, and it is written down here rather than left for someone to discover.

LevelBehaviourTokenWhat sits here
0Flat--elevation-flatPage, body content, buttons, inputs, tabs, table rows, tags, badges, accordions, alerts.
1Raised--elevation-raisedA part that moves inside a control that does not — switch knob, slider thumb, the selected item in a segmented control. Also a deliberately raised card.
2Sticky--elevation-stickyChrome that stays put while content scrolls under it: the top bar, a sticky table header, a sticky order summary.
3Overlay--elevation-overlayAnchored floating UI: menu, popover, tooltip, every select and combobox list, date and time pickers.
4Modal--elevation-modalSurfaces that take the page away and need a scrim: dialog, drawer.
5Transient--elevation-transientNever at rest: toast, and an item being dragged. Same shadow as modal; it clears a dialog by --z-toast.

Flat is the default, not the absence of a decision

Most of an interface carries no shadow at all. A 1px border does the containing in dense UI — --elevation-border exists as a named option for exactly that — and shadows are saved for surfaces that genuinely float. A grid where every card is raised has no hierarchy left to read.

Never stack shadows

In nested layers only the outermost surface carries one. A card inside a dialog is flat; a list inside a popover is flat; the panel of an open accordion is flat. Two shadows in one stack read as noise rather than as two depths.

Surface, shadow and stacking order are one decision

A shadow on the wrong surface colour reads as a rendering bug. --elevation-overlay goes with --surface-card and --z-dropdown, and none of the three is chosen without the other two — which is why the shadows and the z-index band live in the same file.

Toast5 · --elevation-transient
Dialog, drawer4 · --elevation-modal
Menu, popover, tooltip3 · --elevation-overlay
Top bar, on scroll2 · --elevation-sticky
Switch knob, raised card1 · --elevation-raised
Page content0 · --elevation-border

The real tokens, rendered. Level 0 is drawn with --elevation-border here so it has an edge to show; in a product it carries nothing at all.

Two gaps, and one disagreement

Level 2 was missing entirely. The top bar is sticky and had no elevation treatment at all, so content scrolled under it with nothing marking the seam. A bar earns its shadow only once content is actually underneath it — flat at the top of the page, --elevation-sticky after that.

Level 5 has no drag state. Nothing here can be dragged yet, so --elevation-transient covers only the toast. When a draggable component arrives, the item takes this level plus a 1–2° tilt and returns to its rest level on drop.

The inline alert sits a level lower than the consensus. Material 3 puts a banner at 1, Polaris at its 200, and this system keeps it at 0. An alert here already carries a tint, a border and an icon; adding depth on top would make a message about the page look like it floats over the page. The same component floating as a toast is a different question and takes level 5.

Interaction states

A surface that lifts on hover is claiming it can be picked up or opened. If it cannot be, it must not lift. Hover moves one level; press returns to rest and never adds shadow.

SituationReach for
A busy or photographic background behind a cardShadow — a hairline will not separate it

The scrim is 56% rather than something lighter because the page behind a dialog is a marketplace with product photography in it, and a thinner scrim leaves it competing for attention.

Shadows on coloured and dark surfaces

A shadow works by darkening what is behind it, so its strength depends entirely on what that is. Every level here mixes from #0A0C0A, which is tuned for the page and card surfaces — and stops working as the ground gets darker.

GroundShadow reads asVerdict
--surface-card1.43:1Works — this is what the ramp was tuned against
--surface-page1.42:1Works
--surface-brand1.22:1Weak. A card dropped on the green hero needs its own edge, not a heavier shadow.
--surface-inverse1.02:1Invisible. There is nothing left to darken.

Which turned up a live one: the tooltip sits on --surface-inverse and is documented as level 3, and its shadow was resolving at 1.02:1 against its own ground. It was paying for a shadow it never received — and in the interactive layer it had no box-shadow at all, so the React component and the CSS disagreed on top of that.

The fix is the mechanism, not a bigger number: a dark surface gets its edge drawn. --elevation-edge-on-dark is an inset hairline in --alpha-white-16, and it composes with the level rather than replacing it, so a tooltip still carries overlay elevation for any ground that can show it.

Shadow only — no edge Shadow plus drawn edge

Both on --surface-inverse. The first is the level the docs promised; the second is the level you can actually see.

The same mechanism is what a dark theme will need across the board — lighter surface per level, stronger shadow, and a hairline on overlays. None of that exists yet, and it is listed as a gap rather than described as if it did.

Stacking order

120ToastL5
101Dialog, drawerL4
100Scrim—
70TooltipL3
60Menu, popover, listsL3
50Top barL2

Shadow says which layer something looks like it is on; z-index decides which layer it renders on. Define both per level or they drift — and they had: a dropdown sat at 40 while the sticky header sat at 50, so a select opened near the top of the page rendered behind the header. Both now come from tokens in tokens/elevation.css, next to the shadows they belong with.

TokenValueLevelLayer
--z-base00Page content
--z-raised11A focused control lifting above its neighbour's border
--z-sticky502Top bar, sticky rails
--z-dropdown603Menu, popover, every select and combobox list
--z-tooltip703Above a dropdown, because it can describe one
--z-backdrop100not a levelThe scrim — it dims, it does not float
--z-modal1014Dialog, drawer — one above their own scrim
--z-toast1205Above everything, including a dialog

Two traps worth knowing. A transform, filter or opacity below 1 creates a new stacking context, so a child's z-index is then measured against its parent rather than the page — which is why the interactive card's 2px lift must never wrap a dropdown. And an overlay inside a scrolling container clips against it. Both are solved by rendering overlays at the document root rather than by raising numbers.
