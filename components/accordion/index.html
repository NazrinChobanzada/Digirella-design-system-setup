Components

Accordion New

A vertical stack of headers that reveal their content on press. Useful for shortening a long page — and risky, because hidden content is often unread content.

Examples

Both accordions below work. The first closes the open panel when another opens; the second lets them stack.

Single

Orders placed before 3pm ship the same day. Anything later goes out the next business day.
You can return anything unused and in undamaged packaging within 14 days of delivery, no reason needed.
The invoice is emailed to your account address as soon as the order ships. You can also download it from the Orders page.

Multiple

147.5 × 71.5 × 7.85 mm, 206 g.

Handset, USB-C cable, original packaging and documentation.

The height animates with grid-template-rows, so panels of any length open smoothly without measuring anything in JavaScript.

Overview

An accordion trades scanning for scrolling. That's a good trade when someone needs one of many answers, and a bad one when they need all of them.

When to use

  • FAQs — people arrive with one question and want to find it, not read the rest.
  • Secondary product detail — specifications, shipping terms, return policy under a product description.
  • Advanced settings — options most people never change.

When not to use

Don't hide content people need in order to decide. If a shopper has to open three panels to compare shipping cost, delivery time, and return window, the accordion has made the page shorter and the task longer.

Hidden content is skipped content. It doesn't get found by Ctrl+F in a closed panel, it's easy to miss when printing, and it's invisible to someone skim-reading. Before hiding something, ask whether the page is genuinely too long or just feels untidy.
Instead of an accordionUse
Switching between peer viewsTabs
One short block of extra detailAn inline disclosure
Content people need side by sideJust show it
Long, distinct topicsSeparate pages

Single versus multiple

Single mode closes the open panel when another opens. It keeps the page short and predictable, and it's the default.

Multiple mode lets panels stack open. Use it when someone might reasonably want to compare two sections — but expect the page to grow past a screen height quickly.

ModeUse when
SinglePanels are alternatives. FAQs, help topics, a settings group.
MultiplePanels are complementary and might be read together. Specifications, filter groups.

Anatomy

Free over ₼500, otherwise ₼49.
  • 1 Trigger — the whole row, not just the text.
  • 2 Label — a question or a noun phrase.
  • 3 Chevron — rotates 180° when open.
  • 4 Panel — content, indented to align with the label.

The entire header row is the trigger. A chevron-only target is small, easy to miss, and fails the reasonable-target-size expectation on touch.

Content

Headers should say what's inside without being opened. "Shipping and returns" works; "More information" doesn't, because it makes people open panels to find out whether they wanted them.

Do name the content so it can be found without opening.
Don't use vague headers that force a hunt.

Adding a count or a summary to the header helps when it saves an open — "Shipping · from ₼49" answers the common case in place.

Behaviour

Keeping state

If someone opens a panel, follows a link, and comes back, the panel should still be open. Losing that state on every navigation makes an accordion feel hostile on a long support page.

Deep linking

Give each panel an id and open the matching one when the URL carries its fragment. Without it, a link to a specific answer drops people at a page of closed headers.

Nesting

Don't nest accordions. Two levels of hiding means someone has to guess twice, and the indentation stops reading as hierarchy. If the content needs that much structure, it needs its own page.

How many

Three to eight panels. Fewer than three and the machinery costs more than it saves — just show the content. More than eight and the closed headers become their own wall to scan.

Style

A bordered container with hairline dividers between items. The only moving parts are the chevron and the panel height.

An open header gets no fill. Rotating the chevron and revealing the panel is enough of a signal, and a tinted header competes with the content it just exposed.

The panel has no top padding — the header's bottom padding already provides the gap, and doubling it makes an open panel look detached from its header.

Type

Headers are --type-body-size at 600, left-aligned. Panel text is --type-body-size at 400 with a var(--line-height-normal) line height. Headers deliberately match body size rather than stepping up — an accordion is a list of peers, not a page outline.

Motion

The panel animates grid-template-rows from 0fr to 1fr over --duration-normal. This is what lets a panel of any length animate correctly without measuring its height in JavaScript first.

.acc__panel { display: grid; grid-template-rows: 0fr;
  transition: grid-template-rows var(--duration-normal) var(--ease-standard); }
.acc__panel[data-expanded="true"] { grid-template-rows: 1fr; }
.acc__inner { overflow: hidden; }

The chevron rotates 180° over the same duration. Under prefers-reduced-motion both drop to 1ms — the panel still opens, it just stops travelling.

Elevation

None. The accordion sits flat on the page like a card, and gains no shadow when open. Elevation in this system means layering — a popover above content — and an accordion never leaves the flow.

Code

Three nested elements per panel. The extra wrapper is what makes the height animation work.

Plain HTML

Outside React, interactive.js drives it. Put data-acc on the container and the runtime handles the rest — no initialisation call.

<div class="acc" data-acc="single">
  <div class="acc__item">
    <button type="button" class="acc__trigger" aria-expanded="false">
      When does an order ship?<span class="acc__chev">▼</span>
    </button>
    <div class="acc__panel" data-expanded="false">
      <div class="acc__inner">
        <div>Orders placed before 3pm ship the same day.</div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
AttributeEffect
data-acc="single"On the container. Use "multi" to allow several open.
aria-expandedOn the trigger. The runtime keeps it in sync; set the initial value yourself.
data-expandedOn the panel. Must match the trigger's initial aria-expanded.
The three-element nesting isn't decorative. The animation runs on the outer .acc__panel grid, and .acc__inner supplies the overflow: hidden that clips the content mid-transition. Flatten it and panels will jump open instead of sliding.

Persisting state

Neither the component nor the runtime stores which panels are open. Keep it in the URL when a panel is worth linking to, and in your own state layer otherwise.

document.addEventListener('click', (e) => {
  const t = e.target.closest('.acc__trigger');
  if (!t) return;
  const open = t.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
  history.replaceState(null, '', open ? `#${t.dataset.id}` : ' ');
});

Accessibility

The pattern rests on one attribute: aria-expanded on a real button. Everything else follows from that.

Keyboard

KeyResult
TabMoves to the next trigger, or into an open panel's content if it has focusable elements.
Enter or SpaceToggles the panel.

Arrow keys are deliberately not bound. Unlike tabs, accordion headers aren't a single composite widget — they're a list of independent buttons, and Tab is the expected way through them. Adding arrow navigation here would break the mental model people bring from every other accordion.

Markup requirements

  • The trigger is a <button>. A <div> with a click handler isn't focusable, isn't announced as a control, and doesn't respond to Space.
  • aria-expanded reflects the panel state and updates on every toggle. This is what a screen reader announces as "collapsed" or "expanded".
  • If headers are section headings in the page outline, wrap each button in an <h3> so heading navigation still works. Wrap the button — don't put the button inside the heading's text.
<h3 style="margin:0">
  <button type="button" class="acc__trigger" aria-expanded="false">
    Return terms<span class="acc__chev" aria-hidden="true">▼</span>
  </button>
</h3>

The chevron takes aria-hidden="true". It duplicates what aria-expanded already says, and without it screen readers announce a stray character after every header.

Hidden content and assistive technology

A closed panel must be genuinely hidden, not just visually collapsed. The grid-template-rows: 0fr plus overflow: hidden combination removes it from the accessibility tree in current browsers, so a screen reader skips it and Tab can't land on anything inside.

Verify this if you restyle the panel. Collapsing with height: 0 and no overflow: hidden leaves focusable content reachable but invisible — someone tabbing through lands on a link they can't see, which is worse than no accordion at all.

Find on page

Content in a closed panel isn't found by the browser's find. Chromium's hidden="until-found" fixes this by auto-opening the panel on a match, and degrades harmlessly elsewhere. Worth adding on support and FAQ pages, where searching is exactly how people navigate.

The divider is exempt because it separates rather than defines: removing it entirely would leave the accordion fully usable, so WCAG treats it as decoration.

Target size

The trigger spans the full width of the container and is 52px tall, well past the 24px minimum in WCAG 2.2 2.5.8. This is the main reason the whole row is the target rather than just the chevron.

What you still have to do

  • Keep aria-expanded accurate if you drive the accordion yourself.
  • Mark the chevron aria-hidden.
  • Wrap triggers in headings when they're part of the page outline.
  • Don't hide anything required to complete a task.
