A field for values you will do arithmetic on. The field is always typeable; the controls on its right are an accelerator, not the only way in.

Overview

A number input holds a quantity — something you will add up, multiply or compare. That is narrower than it sounds: a postcode, a card number and a phone number look numeric but are digit strings. They keep leading zeros, never need incrementing, and belong in Formatted input.

The test is whether adding one to the value would mean anything. If it would not, this is not the control.

Principles

Three ideas decide almost every number field.

Whole or fractional decides the control

The split is not big numbers against small ones. It is whether the value can be counted or has to be measured.

A quantity of 14 and a quantity of 1,400 are both whole — stepping them by one is meaningful even when nobody would press the button that many times, and the field accepts a typed value either way. A price of 32,400.50 is fractional: stepping it by one is arbitrary, because the unit that matters is the qəpik, not the manat. Fractional values are typed, so they get no controls.

ValueControlWhy
WholeField with subtract and addOne is a real unit — one more item, one more guest, one more seat.
FractionalField only, with a unit suffixThere is no obvious step. Incrementing a price by 1 skips 99 meaningful values.

Typing is never secondary

Even with controls attached, the field is a field. Someone ordering fourteen types fourteen; nobody presses plus thirteen times. The controls exist to make small adjustments cheap, and a control that removes the keyboard has taken more than it gave.

That also means the value is clamped on blur, not on every keystroke. Correcting someone mid-number — snapping 1 to the minimum of 10 while they are still typing 15 — makes the field unusable.

The unit follows the number

Prices, weights and percentages read with the symbol after the digits here: 32.400 ₼, not ₼32.400. The suffix sits inside the field boundary so the pair reads as one value, and it is not focusable — it repeats what the label already established.

A prefix is for the rare case where the symbol is part of the value rather than a unit describing it — the + in an international dialling code, or the # in a reference. Those characters travel with the data. A currency symbol does not: it describes the number, and here it describes it from the right.

Ranges

A range is two number inputs that know about each other: a minimum and a maximum, where neither may cross the other. The pair is the control, not each field alone.

Three rules keep a range usable. Wrap the pair in a role="group" with one label, so it is announced as a single control. Allow either side to be empty rather than forcing both. And when someone enters a minimum above the maximum, swap the two values instead of erroring — that is almost always what they meant.

Where the person needs to explore the band before landing on it, pair the two fields with a two-handle slider — see Slider below.

Slider

A slider is a number input with a second way in — the same value, the same bounds, driven either by dragging or by typing. It comes in two forms, and the choice is whether the answer is one figure or two.

Single value

One handle, for a ceiling or a level: a maximum price, a discount, a volume.

Two handles, for a band with a floor and a ceiling. This is the form a price filter needs — "under 7,500" and "between 500 and 7,500" are different questions, and only the second is a range.

Both handles are real range inputs, so Tab reaches each one and the arrow keys move it. Try tabbing to a handle and holding the left arrow.

Four rules keep a range honest.

  • The handles may meet but never cross. Each one's limit is the other's current position, so the pair can close to zero width but cannot invert.
  • Typing a minimum above the maximum swaps them. That is almost always what the person meant, and an error message here teaches nothing.
  • The pair shares one label. A role="group" with a single aria-labelledby, so it is announced as one control; each handle keeps its own name inside it.
  • Show the bounds. The scale under the track says what the extremes are — without it, a handle at the far left could mean zero or could mean the lowest available price.

Use a slider alone, with no fields, only when the exact number genuinely does not matter — brightness, zoom. In a form, a slider without fields forces someone to hunt for a value they already know.

When a limit exists because of stock or policy, say so beside the control. A plus button that stops working with no explanation reads as a bug rather than a rule.

Defaults

Default to 0, not 1. A pre-filled 1 looks like a helpful shortcut and behaves like a trap: it is easy to scroll past, and because the field is not empty it satisfies a required check. The form submits, no error appears, and a quantity nobody chose is now in your data.

0 fails the same required check honestly. The person is stopped, sees the field, and makes the decision — which is the whole point of asking.

0 is visibly unanswered, so it cannot pass unnoticed.
1 submits silently even when nobody looked at it.

The exception is a control where the item's presence already answers the question. A cart row starts at 1 because the item is in the cart — the quantity is confirming something, not deciding it. Everywhere the quantity is a decision, start at 0.

Empty and zero

Empty and zero are different answers and the field should keep them apart. Empty means "not decided"; zero means "none". Do not fill an empty field with 0 on blur — that turns a blank into a decision the person never made.

Rounding and precision

Decide what happens to extra decimals before someone finds out by accident. Round on blur, not on input — truncating the third decimal while the second is still being typed is disorienting. State the precision in the hint when it is not obvious from the unit.

Accessibility

The controls are the part that fails, because they look like two buttons and have to behave as part of one field.

Field and controls

A single widget wearing three elements.

Values, units and ranges

Where a number needs context to mean anything.

  • 1.3.1A range pair is wrapped in role="group" with a single label; each field and each handle keeps its own name. high risk
  • 2.1.1Each slider handle is reachable by Tab and moved by the arrow keys. Building a range from divs and drag handlers loses this. high risk
  • 4.1.2Each handle reports aria-valuenow plus its effective bounds — the minimum handle's maximum is the other handle's current position, not the global maximum.
  • 2.5.8Handles are 18px drawn but sit in a 22px row; keep clear space around the track so neighbouring targets do not overlap.
  • 1.1.1The unit suffix is decorative and hidden from assistive technology — the unit belongs in the label, where it is announced once.
  • 3.3.1A limit that blocks entry is stated in text beside the field, not implied by a dead control.
  • 3.3.3An out-of-range value gets a message naming the limit — "Maximum 3 per order".
  • 1.3.5inputmode set to numeric or decimal so a phone brings up the right keypad.

What you still have to do

  • Give each control a name that includes what is being counted.
  • Keep aria-valuenow in sync on every change, typed or pressed.
  • Put the unit in the label, not only in the suffix — the suffix is hidden from screen readers.
  • Explain any limit in text beside the field.
  • Group the two halves of a range and label the pair once.
